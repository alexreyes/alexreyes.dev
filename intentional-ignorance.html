Intentional ignorance


What comes to mind when you think of ignorance?


Probably nothing good. I remember being eight years old and being told by adults that reading the news made you more informed, and therefore a better person. I remember being told that people who didn’t read the news were somehow worse off because of it. I vividly remember being called “ignant” in middle school and feeling insulted.


So ignorance is a bad thing. But I guess this makes this next statement extra odd: a couple of months ago I decided to be ignorant. On purpose.


I'd been feeling overwhelmed. Reading about wars, climate change, and politics had gotten to me. Consuming information about faraway events led to very close feelings of sadness, helplessness, and fear.


So I decided I wanted to quit the news.


At first, the idea of quitting the news felt scary. Did ignoring the news make me a bad person? I really did wonder about this. If I didn’t know about and didn't speak out about violence in Gaza, was I somehow complicit? People say “Silence is violence”. Is ignorance violence?


The idea that you must be informed and you must voice your opinion on every news event to “be a good person” feels like a big unquestioned assumption of my generation (gen z). Deciding to quit the news felt like I would be going against that.


But I couldn't hold on to the weight of global suffering anymore. I wanted to focus on what was within my control instead of oscillating between feelings of doom and anxiety at the whims of the news cycle. So I decided to try an experiment in intentional ignorance.



Step 1 of the experiment was figuring out how to quit the news. Quitting the news is hard. It was everywhere — on my phone, at the pizza shop, in conversations I’d eavesdrop on. I quickly realized that the news is deeply woven into the fabric of everyday life and it wasn’t something I could easily tear out.


I thought about it, and realized that social media was my biggest source of news consumption. So I committed to quitting social media. Mostly [1].


The challenging part about my previous attempts to quit social media was that I always ended up with a replacement addiction. When I quit Twitter I ended up on Reddit. When I quit Reddit, I migrated to Hacker News.


This time though, it had to be different. After several failed attempts, I found something that worked. Instead of scrolling, I started to ask questions to AI assistants. I explored all the things I was curious about but never had time to investigate. From basic questions like why the sky is blue, to historical questions about what caused the Renaissance, to complicated questions like what the foundational crisis in mathematics was about.


I had found a new outlet for my information addiction. Although I never succeeded at just “using my phone less”, I wasn’t reading the news, and that was good enough. As my focus shifted from current events to explorations of the past, something unexpected started to happen.



I started to be curious about the world again. I started to see possibilities. I started to wonder, dream, and get excited. I vividly remember going for a run around this time, and feeling absolutely present. The sun was shining and a cool breeze nurtured my skin as I took in the world with a clear mind. The world wasn’t ending. Things weren’t terrible. The day was bright and sunny. The future felt that way too.


Along with this clarity came a newfound mental freedom. I had space to evaluate what I believed. With no topics off limits, I found myself exploring history, math, science — basically anything I was remotely interested in. It felt like I was connecting dots, being creative again. I started to write, to read books, to take salsa classes. I felt like I was learning. Growing.


It all felt like a personal renaissance.


But like all honeymoons, this one couldn’t last forever. And after a few weeks of bliss, I started seeing the cracks in my methods. Turning off social media and trying my hardest to ignore the news meant I was disconnecting from a version of the world that most people around me were still immersed in. By walking away from that, I was turning myself into a bit of an alien.




I started to feel like I was missing out on everything. I felt like a raft lost at sea, with nowhere to anchor myself anymore. I wasn’t “in the know”. Being out of the loop isn’t known as a nice feeling, and I started to realize that I was always out of the loop.


This became really vivid when I hung out with some friends who are working on startups. We were all talking, and they were going back and forth discussing the latest AI models, the “hottest” AI startups, and sharing inside jokes I only barely understood.


At that moment, I realized I was experiencing a strange role reversal. Four years ago, when my brother (who doesn’t work in tech) came to visit, he sat quietly while my friends and I talked about things that only made sense to people chronically on tech twitter. After listening for a while, he looked at me and said “it feels like you guys are speaking a different language”.


Four years later, I felt like my brother — an outsider who stumbled onto a strange world with a strange language. Except this strange world used to be my home!


This feeling of disconnection followed me outside of tech. At my run club, when I’d chat with strangers, conversations often turned to current events or politics. When I would say “oh I didn’t hear about that, can you give me a recap?” I would oftentimes be met with confused looks. I get it. It is odd to live in 2025 and not have heard about the latest thing Trump did, the most recent school shooting, or the latest influencer drama.


“What kind of rock do you live under?” I started to wonder the same thing.


Choosing to quit the news meant putting on blinders. I had succeeded in moving my mental spotlight away from the current moment, and in return I discovered new interests and experienced mental clarity and a freedom I had never felt before. But there were very real social costs to this choice, and now I wondered if it was worth it.



Now, as I reflect on this experiment that started a few months ago, I find it hard to say whether the outcomes were “good” or “bad”. It’s easy to find articles online that sound too much like “I quit social media and now my life is amazing!”, and I think that oversimplifies my experience.


What I've come to realize is that ‘disconnecting’ is a more difficult choice than I would’ve expected. When I disconnected from the news and social media, I felt like I took myself out of a common reality that most people around me experience. And while it was freeing, it was also isolating in ways I hadn’t expected.


The question that haunted me at the beginning—whether ignorance makes me complicit or somehow "bad" — now seems like the wrong question entirely.


In the end, I haven’t found the ideal “middle path” that allows for being plugged in and still allowing for peace. I’m still living under a bit of a rock, and still feeling like a bit of an alien. I’m still trying to make sense of the world.


But, it wasn’t all bad. The freedom I found from being intentionally ignorant created space to nurture and grow my own curiosity. And that, to me, has been priceless. By looking away from the present moment, I had a lot of fun rediscovering timeless questions that ultimately expanded my world.


So what’s the review? Well, it was a worthwhile experiment. Do I think more people should try it? I don’t know. But, I do know that choosing to be intentionally ignorant changed me in some very unexpected ways, and for that alone, I’m glad I did it.



[1]. According to screen time for the last week, I spent average of: 8.7 min/day on Reddit, 5.71 min/day on Hacker News, and 0 min/day on Twitter or Instagram. This constitutes what I consider mostly quitting.
